## S01 · Your Legal Structure Defines Your Venture  `0:04.50`

Every venture starts with a choice that most founders rush through: the legal structure. That choice decides who is on the hook when things go wrong, how the profits are taxed, and who gets to make the decisions. In this study guide, we'll cover three things. First, the business entities. Second, how corporations and LLCs are governed and owned. And third, leverage, the supply chain, and cognitive bias. Let's begin.

## S02 · Sole Proprietorship  `0:31.67`

Start with the simplest structure: the sole proprietorship. One individual owns it and runs it. It's easy to form, but there is no wall between you and the business. The owner has unlimited personal liability for every business debt and obligation. Profits and losses are reported directly on the owner's personal tax return. And that is exactly why it's considered a very imprudent way to conduct business.

## S03 · General Partnership  `0:56.88`

Next, partnerships. A general partnership is a for-profit business of two or more persons, governed by state law. And persons can mean individuals, groups of individuals, companies, or even corporations. Every partner shares in the profits, the losses, and control of the business. The catch: all partners have unlimited personal liability, and they are jointly and severally liable. That means a creditor can pursue any one partner for the entire debt. For taxes, the partnership itself is not a taxable entity. Income passes through to the partners, in proportion to their share of the profits.

## S04 · Limited Partnership: Fees  `1:32.47`

A limited partnership has at least one general partner, and one or more limited partners. It's the classic structure for private investment funds. The general partner, or GP, typically charges two fees. The first is the management fee: an annual payment from the limited partners to cover the fund's operating expenses. A common structure, especially in the early investment period, is two percent of committed capital. The second is carried interest: the GP's share of the fund's investment profits.

## S05 · Carried Interest & the Hurdle  `2:03.82`

The most common arrangement is an eighty-twenty split. Eighty percent of the profits go to the limited partners, and twenty percent go to the GP as carried interest. But carry is performance-based. The GP only earns it after the LPs have received their initial capital back, and sometimes a minimum return first, called the hurdle rate. Hurdle rates often range from seven to ten percent per year.

## S06 · LP Liability & Taxes  `2:29.77`

On liability: general partners have unlimited personal liability, and they manage the business. Limited partners are liable only up to their capital contribution, and they don't participate in daily operations. In fact, a limited partner who gets too close to decision-making may be treated as a general partner, depending on state law. For taxes, a limited partnership is a pass-through entity. General partners pay self-employment tax on their share, and their losses are treated as active. Limited partners' income is passive. Their losses can generally only offset other passive income, and they don't pay self-employment tax.

## S07 · Distributions & Basis  `3:06.32`

One more rule to remember. Distributions of cash or property are generally not taxable, unless they exceed the partner's adjusted basis in the partnership. Any excess is taxed as a capital gain. And a partner can't deduct losses beyond their basis. Those extra losses are carried forward.

## S08 · Limited Liability Partnership  `3:24.86`

A limited liability partnership, or LLP, combines the management flexibility of a partnership with corporate-like liability protection. It's common for professional firms: lawyers, accountants, and doctors. It requires state registration and a formal partnership agreement. Partners are generally not responsible for another partner's malpractice or debts, but they remain liable for their own actions. And like a traditional partnership, profits and losses pass through to the partners' individual tax returns.

## S09 · Corporations  `3:55.22`

Now, corporations. The corporate form has two big advantages: limited liability, and unlimited duration. A partnership can dissolve when a partner withdraws, goes bankrupt, or dies. Limited partnerships share that problem with their general partners. But a corporation can go on in perpetuity, regardless of what happens to its investors.

## S10 · When Limited Liability Fails  `4:16.18`

Limited liability has exceptions. If you personally guarantee a loan or a lease, you're personally liable if the company can't pay. You're always responsible for your own wrongful acts, like fraud or theft from the business. Breaching the duties set out in the bylaws or operating agreement can create personal liability. And in rare cases, courts can pierce the corporate veil, ignoring the company's separate status when it isn't properly maintained, or when it's used to commit fraud.

## S11 · C-Corp vs S-Corp  `4:45.32`

The C corporation is the standard structure. There are no limits on the number or type of shareholders, it can issue multiple classes of stock, and it gives owners the strongest liability protection. The trade-off is double taxation. The corporation's profits are taxed, and shareholders are taxed again on dividends. The S corporation is designed for small, non-public companies. A business first forms as a C corp, then files Form twenty-five fifty-three with the IRS. It's limited to one hundred shareholders, generally US citizens or residents, and only one class of stock. Profits pass through to the owners. And remember this for the exam: venture firms cannot invest in S corps.

## S12 · Limited Liability Company  `5:26.26`

The limited liability company, or LLC, blends a partnership and a corporation. Instead of shareholders, it has members, who own membership units, not stock, under an operating agreement. Members generally enjoy limited liability. By default, a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship, on Schedule C of the owner's personal return. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. It files Form ten sixty-five and issues each member a Schedule K one. And any LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corp or a C corp instead.

## S13 · PC & PLLC  `6:03.61`

Licensed professionals, like doctors, lawyers, architects, and accountants, have two more options. The professional corporation, or PC, offers corporate liability protection, with state rules ensuring that only qualified professionals own it. The professional LLC, or P LLC, adds the flexibility and pass-through taxation of an LLC. In both cases, you're protected from a partner's negligence, but never from your own.

## S14 · The Entity Landscape  `6:32.39`

Here's the whole landscape in one view. Liability runs from unlimited for sole proprietors and general partners, to limited for corporations and LLCs. And taxation splits between pass-through entities, and the double taxation of the C corp.

## S15 · Governance & Articles of Incorporation  `6:50.41`

Section nine: governance, ownership, and control. Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. The goal is accountability, fairness, transparency, and efficiency. It starts with the articles of incorporation, also called the corporate charter. It's filed with the state and becomes a public record, listing the company's name, purpose, stock structure, board of directors, and registered agent. Filing it creates the corporation as a distinct legal entity.

## S16 · Bylaws  `7:23.99`

Bylaws are the corporation's internal rulebook. They're legally binding and adopted by the board, but, unlike the articles, they are not filed with the state. Bylaws cover the board's size, elections, and removal. Officers' appointments and duties. Meeting procedures, like notice, quorum, and voting. Shareholder rights and stock transfers. Conflicts of interest. And how to amend the bylaws themselves, which often requires a supermajority.

## S17 · Internal Controls: COSO  `7:52.14`

Internal controls are different. They're the policies and procedures that safeguard assets, ensure reliable financial reporting, promote efficient operations, and ensure compliance. The COSO framework has five components. Control environment. Risk assessment. Control activities. Information and communication. And monitoring. Here's the shortcut: bylaws are the who and the what. Internal controls are the how.

## S18 · Board vs Officers  `8:18.50`

Shareholders elect the board of directors. Directors owe a fiduciary duty of care and loyalty to the corporation. They set strategy, approve major policies, appoint the officers, and make sure the company follows the law. But they don't run day-to-day operations. That's the officers' job. The CEO, the CFO, and the Secretary execute the board's strategy, manage daily activities, and report back on financial performance.

## S19 · Categories of Stock  `8:46.87`

A share is a unit of ownership. The number of shares you own sets your percentage of the company. Know these five categories. Authorized stock is the total the charter allows. Issued stock has been sold or distributed. Unissued stock is authorized, but held back for future raises or employee stock plans. Outstanding stock is held by investors and can be voted. And treasury stock was issued, then bought back by the company. Shareholders may get voting rights, dividends when the board declares them, a residual claim on assets in a liquidation, the right to inspect records, and the ability to transfer their shares.

## S20 · Common vs Preferred  `9:23.00`

Common stock usually carries one vote per share, but it's last in line. Dividends come after preferred, and in a liquidation, common holders get whatever is left after creditors and preferred holders are paid. Preferred stock gets priority on fixed dividends and on assets, usually without voting rights. It can be callable, cumulative, or convertible into common. And some private companies create Class A and Class B shares to give founders more voting power.

## S21 · Participating vs Non-Participating  `9:52.98`

Two types of preferred matter most in venture deals. Non-participating preferred must choose: take the liquidation preference, or convert to common for a pro rata share. Not both. This is the more common type in US venture capital. Participating preferred takes its preference, and then also shares in what's left. A double return, and less common. Here's an illustration. An investor puts in ten million dollars for twenty percent, and the company sells for thirty million. Non-participating takes ten million. Participating takes ten million, plus twenty percent of the remaining twenty. Fourteen million.

## S22 · LLC Governance  `10:31.60`

L L Cs work differently. Members can be individuals, other businesses, or even foreign entities. An LLC can be member-managed, where all members run the business, or manager-managed, where members appoint managers who may even be outsiders. The operating agreement defines everything: ownership and contributions, management, profit allocation, voting, membership changes, and dissolution. Units come in classes, often named Class A, B, and C. They can be common or preferred, voting or non-voting. And there are capital interests, tied to current assets, versus profits interests, which grant a share of future profits without a stake in today's assets.

## S23 · Understanding Leverage  `11:13.99`

Section ten closes out the fundamentals, starting with leverage. In physics, a lever lets you move a heavy load with less force. In finance, the lever is debt. The load is the company's assets. And the fulcrum is the capital structure: the mix of debt and equity. More debt amplifies the return on equity. But it amplifies the losses too.

## S24 · The Golf Club Analogy  `11:37.15`

Think of a golf bag. The pitching wedge is short and heavy, built for control. That's low leverage: mostly equity, more stable, and a small change in performance has a small effect on returns. The driver is long and light, built for distance. That's high leverage. A small gain in profits can mean a huge jump in return on equity. But a small decline can wipe the equity out, because the interest still has to be paid. The lesson: choose your leverage for the game you're playing. Need control? Use less. Need distance? Use more. But always weigh the risk against the expected return.

## S25 · Players in the Supply Chain  `12:13.71`

Next, the supply chain. Suppliers provide raw materials and components. Manufacturers develop and produce the goods, bearing high costs before any profit. Distributors buy in bulk and move products into the right channels. Wholesalers sell large quantities to retailers, bridging mass production and retail. Retailers sell to the end consumer, whose demand drives the entire chain. Alongside them, agents and brokers arrange deals without owning the goods. Sales reps extend a producer's sales effort. And third-party logistics providers handle warehousing and transport.
